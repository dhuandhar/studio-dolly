The First Collection

Rangvan

रंगवन

Ten small tiles, ten small myths — a menagerie painted onto black, the way these stories have always been told: in colour, from memory, one creature at a time. Every collection at Studio Dolly starts from a different spark; this one started in the wild.

Deer

Sanskrit poets measured beauty against her eyes — mriga-nayani, "deer-eyed" — a creature that listens long before it is ever seen.

Elephant

The elephant lends its form to Ganesha, remover of obstacles — a creature whose memory, in every telling, forgets nothing.

Fish

Vishnu's first descent came as a fish, Matsya, who towed Manu's boat safely through the flood that drowned the world.

Fox

The fox owes its reputation to the Panchatantra, where wit always outlasts strength, and the smallest animal in the room is the one worth watching.

Owl

Lakshmi rides an owl by night — an unglamorous choice, until you remember it's the one creature that can find wealth in the dark.

Parakeets

A pair of parakeets was Kamadeva's chosen escort, and across a thousand years of folk tales it's the parrot who narrates, never just watches.

Peacock

The peacock dances only for the first clouds of monsoon, and carries Kartikeya into battle — grief and glory kept in the same feather.

Sun

Surya crosses the sky once a day and is thanked for it every morning — the one god whose worship needs no idol, only sunrise.

Swan

The mythical hamsa is said to drink only the milk from a bowl of milk and water, leaving the water behind — discernment, taught as a party trick.

Turtle

When the gods needed something steady enough to hold up a mountain, Vishnu became a turtle, Kurma, and simply stayed still.

The Second Collection

Warli Katha

वार्ली कथा

Warli painting belongs to no single artist — it's a tradition of the Warli people of the Sahyadri hills, kept for generations in white rice-paste on walls of mud and cow-dung, long before it ever touched a coaster. Thirty-four small scenes, drawn from one continuous village day: dancers circling at dusk, water carried home from the well, a tree that has always been sacred. Where Rangvan invents a myth for each creature, Warli Katha simply keeps watching — the different spark a second collection was always going to bring.

तारपाDance & Music

The tarpa dance is the one unbroken circle in Warli life — dancers moving around a central musician playing the tarpa, a gourd-horn whose single note doesn't stop until the dancers do. Drums, shehnais, and the sunburst overhead all belong to the same afternoon.

Dancing Duo with Drum

Two dancers mid-step, the drum keeping the beat they've already found.

Musician with Shehnai

The shehnai leads the circle, and for once nobody minds being told what to do.

Duo Playing Horns

A pair of tarpa horns played less for melody than for keeping everyone circling the same centre.

Dancers with Sunburst

The dance and the sun above it share the same shape — neither one has a start or an end.

Duo Drummers

Two drums, one rhythm, and the whole village already knows which one to follow.

Horn Player with Companion

One plays, one just walks alongside — the tarpa dance always needs both.

Two Horned Animals

Two animals share the frame, horns up and tails thrown back — the same leaping line Warli hands give a dancer, borrowed here for four legs instead of two.

घरHome & Hearth

A Warli house is drawn the way it's built — bamboo lashed in rows, a domed thatch roof, a ladder leaning up to the loft where the year's grain waits. A tree almost always stands at the door, the way it always has.

Hammock in the Window

A hammock strung across a window — the one hour of the day built for doing nothing.

Hut with Ladder and Hammock

The ladder goes up to the loft; the hammock stays exactly where it is.

Hut with Dancing Figure

Even at home, under its own tree, a reason to dance turns up.

Granary with Ladder

The kudmal — the granary that carries a family to the next harvest — earns a ladder, and a border, of its own.

Domed Hut with Tree

The tree beside the hut isn't decoration; a Warli house is never drawn standing alone.

House with a Ladder

A ladder left leaning against the wall, a window left open — the kind of house drawn mid-chore, not posed for.

House with Windows

Two windows, one tree, and a house that looks lived-in rather than built.

Domed Hut, Tree Beside It

The same pairing, told again — in this tradition a good scene is allowed to repeat.

कामField & Labour

Warli painting was never meant to hang at a distance and be admired — it was made by people who spent their days sowing, grinding grain, and walking to the well, and it shows. Nobody in these scenes is posed; they're mid-task.

Duo with a Pot Between Them

Two figures around a single pot — sharing the water, or sharing the walk it took to fetch it.

Hunter with Bow

Bow drawn, one eye already on the bird that hasn't noticed him yet.

Carrying a Pot Home

The palm tree marks the path home; the pot on the head keeps both hands free.

Bullock Cart

Two yoked pairs, a full cart, and someone riding the load rather than walking beside it — however the harvest gets to market, it gets there.

Pounding Grain

Grain pounded by two people working the same rhythm — alone, it takes twice as long.

Carrying Baskets

Baskets balanced, backs bent the same way — the posture of anyone who's ever carried a harvest home.

Carrying Water Pots

The well is never quite close enough, so the walk back always happens in twos.

Drawing Water from the Well

Rope in hand, the pot already on its way up — the well is the one place everyone in the village eventually stands.

Figure with Bull

A farmer and a bull, drawn exactly the size of their friendship.

Duo Under the Sun

Midday, a pot between them, the sun directly overhead with nowhere else to be.

Sowing the Field

Seed going into the ground the old way — by hand, walking the field's length.

संसारCreatures & Sky

The Warli canvas always left room for whoever else was around — birds crossing the monsoon, a bull grazing at the frame's edge, creatures that don't need a person nearby to matter.

Birds in the Rain

Rain falling as a trail of dots, and two birds crossing it anyway — nothing in a Warli monsoon waits for clear skies.

Horned Bull

A bull standing for no reason except that a bull was there that morning.

Two Birds

Two birds, one trailing the other, both turned the same way — Warli's shorthand for a pair that stays a pair.

Goose with a Small Bird

A goose and a smaller bird sharing one frame, the big shape and the little shape drawn kindly, side by side.

Horse with a Flowing Tail

A horse mid-stride, tail thrown back as if running from nothing in particular.

Three Birds

Three birds instead of the usual two — proof this was drawn from what she actually saw, not a fixed formula.

वृक्षदेवताThe Sacred Tree

Before a house, a dancer, or a field, Warli painting starts with a tree. Vrukshadevata, the tree deity, is drawn as the source everything else grows from — the one motif almost every Warli artist returns to first.

Figure with Flowering Tree

A tree in bloom, and someone standing close enough to notice.

Tree of Life

Roots drawn as carefully as branches — the tree that isn't decoration but the reason the rest of the scene gets to exist.

The Studio

By hand, every time

Each piece starts as a plain tile, is drawn freehand, and is built up layer by layer in colour before being sealed under a glass-smooth coat that can take a hot mug without flinching. Nothing here comes from a mould — look closely across a set and no two are ever quite the same.

Painting is only one of the languages she works in. The same hand turns just as easily to embroidery, needlework, and craft of nearly every kind — always toward the same end, something made exquisitely rather than quickly. Commissions are open across mediums and themes, Rangvan included but never only Rangvan.

A note on care

Wipe clean with a damp cloth. The resin seal takes a hot cup without staining, though it hasn't been tested in a dishwasher — treat each piece the way you'd treat a favourite plate.
